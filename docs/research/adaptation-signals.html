Adaptation Signals

How to read Apple Watch, Whoop and smart-scale data as one system, and why body composition is the last of your metrics to show what training is doing.

48 peer-reviewed sources, each checked against PubMed Figures use simulated data built from published effect sizes, not your records Evidence graded A B C throughout

The short version

  1. Your instinct is right: body composition lags. The body changes on three different clocks. Autonomic signals (HRV, resting HR) react within days. Cardiorespiratory fitness shifts over weeks. Fat and muscle move over months. Read on its own, the slowest clock misleads you in both directions.
  2. The scale rises when hard training starts for physiological reasons. New training expands plasma volume5, refills glycogen (stored with 3–4 g of water per gram7), and swells unaccustomed muscle with fluid for about 3 weeks8. Fat loss continues underneath and shows once that water stops rising.
  3. Detraining hides for the opposite reason. After 2–4 weeks off, blood volume falls about 9% and heart rate at the same workload rises about 11%1. The water lost can offset fat gained, so the scale looks flat while fitness drains. Walking heart rate is the early warning.
  4. Hybrid views work when they align timescales instead of merging them. The core design is one shared time axis with stacked lanes for load, recovery, fitness and composition, plus a model that splits weight into fat and water. A single averaged score can hide which clock is moving.
  5. Every signal needs a personal baseline and an honest noise floor. Wrist VO₂max reads about 4.5 mL/kg/min low39. Apple Watch sleep staging misses about 43 minutes of deep sleep45. Consumer body-fat estimates can be 10 points off13. Trends are usable where absolute numbers are not.
  6. Be modest about "readiness". HRV-guided training improves submaximal physiology, but meta-analyses find no significant VO₂max or performance advantage23,24. Use it to schedule hard days, not to forecast results.

Your body runs on three clocks

Every metric your devices produce is a sensor reading of some physiological system. Those systems adapt at very different speeds. That single fact explains most of the confusion you described.

The fast clock (hours to days) is the autonomic nervous system. After a hard session, parasympathetic activity, which HRV partly measures, needs up to 24 h to recover from easy work, 24–48 h from threshold work and at least 48 h from high-intensity work18. Illness moves this clock too: resting HR, respiratory rate and skin temperature often shift before symptoms appear31,34.

The medium clock (weeks) is cardiorespiratory. Plasma volume expands within days of new training and accounts for nearly all of the early blood-volume gain for 2–4 weeks5. Stop training and it reverses: in trained men, 2–4 weeks off cut plasma volume by 12%, stroke volume by 12% and VO₂max by 6%, while heart rate at the same submaximal workload rose 11%1. VO₂max fell 7% in the first 21 days and settled 16% lower by day 562.

The slow clock (months) is tissue. Body weight responds to a sustained change in energy intake with a half-time of about a year10. Muscle growth measured in the first 3 weeks of resistance training is partly fluid (edema); real hypertrophy shows up later8.

Why the scale lies in both directions

A scale measures total mass: fat, muscle, bone, blood, water, glycogen and whatever is in your gut. Day to day, the last four dominate. Muscle and liver glycogen is stored with 3–4 g of water per gram7, and typical stores run to several hundred grams, so filling or emptying them alone can move the scale by 1–2 kg. The popular "3,500 kcal per pound" rule overestimates how fast tissue changes11.

Scenario 1: "I train hard for two weeks and look fatter"

Three things add mass in the first weeks of a harder block, and all three are adaptations, not setbacks:

  • Plasma volume expansion. Endurance training raises total blood volume (+9%, from 63.6 to 69.3 mL/kg over 10 weeks in one study6). The early gain is almost all plasma and a net increase in total body water5. For an 80 kg person that is roughly half a kilogram.
  • Glycogen. Higher training volume with adequate carbohydrate keeps glycogen stores fuller, with 3–4 g of water per gram7.
  • Muscle swelling from new work. Unaccustomed and eccentric work causes muscle damage and inflammation9. In untrained men, muscle cross-section rose 2.7% by week 3 of resistance training, largely from edema, before real hypertrophy took over (+10.4% by week 10)8. Swollen muscle also looks and feels "softer".

Meanwhile, the extra energy spent training slowly removes fat. Once the fluid gains plateau, usually after 2–4 weeks as the body adapts to the load, the fat loss finally shows on the scale and in the mirror. This is the "keep going two more weeks" payoff you described. Fig. 1's composition lane shows exactly this decomposition.

Scenario 2: "I stop training and don't gain weight, then can't run a mile"

The same mechanisms run in reverse. Plasma volume, glycogen stores and oxidative capacity fall within weeks. Muscle oxidative enzymes decline with a half-time of about 12 days2, and short-term detraining lowers VO₂max and blood volume and impairs endurance performance3. Losing fluid subtracts mass while reduced activity slowly adds fat, so the two can cancel on the scale for weeks. The fitness loss does not hide from heart rate: submaximal HR rose 11% within 2–4 weeks of stopping1. Apple Watch measures a version of this passively every day as walking heart rate average.

Design consequence: never show weight alone. Show it with a real-time trend and uncertainty, beside the load that explains its short-term swings, and beside a fitness marker on the medium clock. A body-fat % reading from a bathroom scale depends on hydration12, so it inherits the same water artifacts.

Hybrid views that paint the whole picture

The goal is what Whoop's Recovery and Strain do, but better: more inputs, every clock represented, uncertainty shown, and each output traceable to the raw data behind it. Four design rules keep hybrids honest:

  • Align, don't merge. Stack metrics as lanes on a shared time axis (Fig. 1). Don't put two units on one chart with two y-axes; that manufactures correlations.
  • Standardize before combining. Put every input in the same currency, deviation from your baseline in SD units, before any weighted sum. Show which inputs drove the result.
  • Decompose, don't just aggregate. Splitting weight into water and fat (Fig. 1, bottom lane) explains more than any combined score.
  • Carry uncertainty. Bands, confidence labels and minimum-data rules. A readiness score from two nights of data is a guess, and should look like one.

The recovery compass

HRV and resting HR usually move in opposite directions, but not always, and the exceptions carry information. Plot 28 days of both as a trail on a two-dimensional map, both in SDs from baseline. Clusters in the upper left mean recovered and adapting. A drift to the lower right means strain or illness. A lower-left drift (both low) matches what Plews and colleagues call HRV "saturation" in heavily trained athletes: HRV falls even as resting HR falls16. That case needs context rather than an alarm.

Sleep barcode and regularity

Sleep duration charts hide the variable that predicts outcomes best: consistency. The Sleep Regularity Index (SRI) is the probability of being in the same state (asleep or awake) at any two times 24 h apart, scaled from −100 to 10043. In 60,977 UK Biobank participants, the more regular sleepers had 20–48% lower all-cause mortality than the least regular fifth, and regularity out-predicted duration44. An actogram (one row per night) makes irregularity visible at a glance, which a bar chart of hours cannot.

Two more hybrids worth building

Fitness drift detector. A single lane that plots the Aerobic Fitness Index (below) against 42-day training load. When load falls and walking HR rises for two consecutive weeks, it flags "fitness slipping" before the scale or your next run would tell you. This is your "one day I can barely run a mile" scenario caught three to four weeks early. Its physiological basis is the rapid blood-volume and stroke-volume loss of detraining1,3.

Personal response finder. Within-person lagged analyses ask which of your behaviors predict your next-day HRV or resting HR: sleep duration, bedtime, late workouts, alcohol tags, travel. Each finding is reported as an effect size with an interval and a minimum sample size, and never as a causal claim. Population effects of everyday stressors on HRV are small (~5%)19, so individual effects need months of data to separate from noise.

Composite scores to build

Seven outputs, each with the question it answers, how it's computed, its evidence grade and what it will not claim. Thresholds marked "starting" are defaults to calibrate against your history.

Readiness

B

Has my nervous system recovered enough for hard training today?

Inputs
Nightly HRV (Whoop RMSSD preferred; Apple SDNN fallback, never mixed), resting HR, respiratory-rate deviation, wrist-temperature deviation, sleep vs need, sleep regularity
Method
Robust z-score of each input vs a 60-day baseline that excludes the last 7 days. HRV log-transformed; its 7-day mean compared with a smallest-worthwhile-change band of ±0.5 SD, as in HRV-guided trials15,21. Weighted sum with HRV the largest weight, and each input's contribution shown.
Output
Ready / Normal / Hold back, with contributors and a confidence level based on data completeness
Evidence
HRV-guided training improves submaximal physiology (g ≈ 0.30) but shows no significant VO₂peak or performance advantage23,24; individual RCTs were positive20–22
Won't claim
That a low score means you shouldn't move at all, or that a high score predicts a PR

Load & Form

B

How much training have I absorbed, and how fresh am I?

Inputs
Raw heart rate during workouts (TRIMP), session RPE × minutes for strength work28, and optionally Apple's effort ratings
Method
Fitness = 42-day exponentially weighted average of daily load; Fatigue = 7-day; Form = Fitness − Fatigue. This is the practical form of the Banister impulse-response model25. The constants are shown and editable.
Output
Fitness, Fatigue and Form lines (Fig. 1, top lane) and the weekly ramp rate in Fitness points
Evidence
The model fits group data, but individual time constants are poorly identified: one elite-swimmer study found a 95% CI of 17–59 days26. Banister-type and variable dose-response models ranked best among six compared27.
Won't claim
Injury risk from an acute:chronic ratio. The causal basis for that isn't established29.

Aerobic Fitness Index

C

Is my engine getting stronger, holding or slipping?

Inputs
VO₂max estimate (28-day median), walking HR average, pace or speed per heartbeat on similar workouts, 1-minute HR recovery, resting HR trend
Method
Direction-aligned z-scores (lower walking HR = better), averaged, with a 90% interval. The trend slope over 21 and 42 days gives the "gaining / holding / slipping" label.
Evidence
Fitness itself is A-grade prognostic37,38; submaximal HR as a training marker is B1,17; the specific composite is new and needs validating
Won't claim
A lab-grade VO₂max. The wrist estimate runs low and agrees only moderately for individuals39.

Body Composition Signal

C

Under the water noise, is tissue actually changing?

Inputs
Daily weight (Wyze), body fat % and lean mass (Wyze via HealthKit), plus Load & Form
Method
A state-space model: weight = tissue + water + noise. Tissue drifts slowly. Water is modelled from training (a Fitness term for plasma and glycogen, and a Fatigue-minus-Fitness term for new-load swelling), with coefficients fitted to your own history. BF% is reported as a 14–28-day median of standardized morning readings only.
Output
Estimated tissue change with an interval, and a "water-inflated" or "water-masked" flag when load explains the scale's movement
Evidence
The physiology is A/B5,7,8; the decomposition model is new and must beat plain smoothing on held-out data before it ships
Won't claim
DXA-grade body fat. Consumer BIA can be ≥10 points off for an individual13.

Adaptation State

C

Taking every clock into account, what is my body doing right now?

Inputs
Fitness trend, Form, HRV and resting-HR z-scores, walking-HR change, nightly illness flags
Method
Ordered rules (starting thresholds): Possible illness if resting HR z ≥ 2 and HRV z ≤ −1 on two consecutive nights. Detraining if Fitness fell >12 in 21 days and walking HR rose >3 bpm. Strained if Form < −15 and HRV z < −0.5. Building if Fitness rose >5 in 14 days with HRV holding. Absorbing if Form < −10. Freshening if Form > 10. Otherwise Maintaining.
Output
The state ribbon in Fig. 1, with a plain-language reason for each day
Evidence
Overreaching and overtraining have no single diagnostic marker30. This is a hypothesis to test against your training history and how you actually performed.
Won't claim
Overtraining syndrome, which is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion30

Illness Watch

B

Is my body responding as if it's fighting something?

Inputs
Nightly resting HR, HRV, respiratory rate, wrist temperature, sleep duration
Method
Requires ≥2 signals to agree (starting thresholds): resting HR z ≥ 2 on two consecutive nights, respiratory rate ≥ +1 breath/min, wrist temperature ≥ +0.5 °C, HRV z ≤ −1.5. Runs a cumulative-sum detector on resting-HR residuals.
Evidence
63% of COVID-19 cases were detectable before symptoms in real time from resting HR31; sensor plus symptom data reached AUC 0.8032; adding temperature raised AUC 4.9% and detected cases 2.75 days before testing34; population resting HR and sleep tracked influenza-like illness35
Won't claim
Any diagnosis. The wording stays "consistent with illness" plus a nudge to rest and watch for symptoms.

Sleep Health

A

Am I getting enough sleep, at consistent times?

Inputs
Sleep and wake windows, duration, sleep midpoint
Method
Duration vs the ≥7 h consensus42, SRI over 14 and 28 days43, midpoint drift; stages shown as secondary context only
Evidence
Duration and regularity are both linked to mortality, regularity more strongly44
Won't claim
Precise deep or REM minutes45

How this compares with Whoop and Apple

Whoop's Recovery (0–100%) draws most of its signal from HRV, with resting HR, sleep performance and respiratory rate adding context52. Its Strain is a logarithmic 0–21 score built from time in personal heart-rate zones, plus a muscular-load estimate for lifting51. Apple's Training Load is a duration-weighted average of workout effort ratings (1–10, auto-estimated for cardio from heart rate, pace, elevation and your profile), comparing the last 7 days with the last 2849,50.

Methods: the statistics behind the pictures

Personal baselines and meaningful change

Every metric is interpreted against your own history. Use a 60-day rolling window that excludes the most recent 7 days, so a sudden change doesn't dilute its own reference. Use robust statistics (median and MAD) so one bad night doesn't distort the baseline:

z = (x − median₆₀) / (1.4826 × MAD₆₀)

A change is only "real" when it exceeds the measurement's typical error, the SD of your repeated measurements47. Buchheit recommends interpreting each change against both the error of measurement and the smallest important change17. For HRV, the usual practical rule is a band of ±0.5 SD around the baseline mean21. Avoid "magnitude-based inference", which doesn't deliver the error rates its proponents claimed48. Use plain intervals instead.

How long until the scale can tell you anything

Suppose day-to-day weight noise is σ ≈ 0.5 kg and you want to detect a real 0.5 kg change. A single reading can't separate the two. Averaging n days shrinks noise to σ/√n, so comparing two 7-day means gives a difference SE of 0.5 × √(2/7) ≈ 0.27 kg. A 0.5 kg change is then only ~1.9 SE, and that assumes no water swing. Add a training-induced water shift of ±1 kg and you need either weeks of data or a model that accounts for water. That is the case for the state-space approach.

State-space smoothing and decomposition (the scale)

tissueₜ = tissueₜ₋₁ + ηₜ η ~ N(0, q), q ≈ 0.03–0.06 kg/day waterₜ = β₁·(Fitnessₜ − Fitness_ref) + β₂·max(0, Fatigueₜ − Fitnessₜ) weightₜ = tissueₜ + waterₜ + εₜ ε ~ N(0, r), r ≈ 0.4–0.6 kg

A Kalman filter gives the real-time trend with an uncertainty band (Fig. 1, weight lane). The β coefficients are fitted to your own history and must beat plain smoothing on held-out weeks before the decomposition is shown as fact. Until then it is labelled "model estimate".

Training impulse and the fitness–fatigue model

TRIMP = minutes × HRr × 0.64·e^(1.92·HRr) (men; 0.86·e^(1.67·HRr) women) HRr = (HR_exercise − HR_rest) / (HR_max − HR_rest) Fitness = Fitnessₜ₋₁ + (Loadₜ − Fitnessₜ₋₁) / 42 Fatigue = Fatigueₜ₋₁ + (Loadₜ − Fatigueₜ₋₁) / 7 Form = Fitness − Fatigue

This is the Banister impulse-response family25. Fitting personal time constants is tempting but ill-conditioned: parameters trade off against each other and confidence intervals are wide26. Fit them with regularization toward the defaults (42 and 7 days) and show the uncertainty.

Change-points, anomalies and N-of-1 analysis

  • Change-point detection (e.g. Bayesian online change-point detection53) marks regime shifts: a new Apple Watch generation, a training block, a move. Baselines reset at confirmed change-points rather than smearing across them.
  • Anomaly detection for illness uses a cumulative-sum statistic on resting-HR residuals, requiring agreement from a second signal to keep false alarms rare.
  • N-of-1 effects use within-person lagged regression with centering and shrinkage toward zero, reported with intervals. Confounding is the default assumption, so language stays associational.

Validation before anything ships

Each score is back-tested on your 2+ years of Health export before it appears in the app. Does "Strained" precede workouts where heart rate ran high for the pace? Does "Detraining" precede drops in the VO₂max estimate? Does "Possible illness" line up with the days you were sick? Every score keeps a log of what it predicted and what happened, so its calibration is visible to you.

Pitfalls and guardrails

  • Absolute numbers from wrist sensors. Use VO₂max, deep-sleep minutes, calories and BIA body fat as trends only39,45,46,13.
  • Mixing sources. Apple SDNN ≠ Whoop RMSSD; Apple daytime resting HR ≠ Whoop sleeping resting HR. The app already keeps them apart by source.
  • Acute:chronic ratios as injury predictors. Widely marketed, causally unsupported29.
  • Regression to the mean. An extreme day is usually followed by a more normal one, whatever you did. Never credit an intervention from one bounce.
  • Alert fatigue. Require agreement between signals and persistence over nights before notifying. A wrong alarm costs more trust than a missed one.
  • Unstandardized weigh-ins. BIA readings are most repeatable at the same time of day14. Morning, after the bathroom, before food or water.
  • Heart-rate recovery comparisons. Clinical HRR thresholds come from standardized treadmill tests36; after-workout HRR only compares within similar workouts.

Inputs that would sharpen the picture most

  • A standardized morning weigh-in: same time, after voiding, before eating. The single biggest improvement to scale data, and free14.
  • One-tap session RPE (0–10) after strength sessions, since heart rate underrates lifting. Session RPE is valid across exercise types28.
  • A monthly benchmark, e.g. 20 minutes at a fixed easy pace or a timed mile walk. Heart rate at a fixed pace is the cleanest fitness signal you can collect at home17.
  • Whoop API for nocturnal RMSSD, Recovery and Strain. HRV is not written to Apple Health54.
  • Lightweight tags: alcohol, late meal, travel, symptoms. These power the personal response finder.
  • An occasional DXA scan (every 3–6 months) to calibrate the scale's body-fat estimate against a reference.

Build roadmap

  • Phase 1: compute core (packages/core). EWMA Fitness/Fatigue/Form, TRIMP from workout heart rate, robust baselines, the Kalman weight filter and SRI. Unit-tested, then back-tested on your full export in data/.
  • Phase 2: visuals (apps/mobile). Native Skia implementations of the adaptation timeline, recovery compass and sleep barcode, with the same interaction model as these figures.
  • Phase 3: Whoop API. Nocturnal RMSSD becomes the Readiness HRV source; compare its Recovery with ours side by side.
  • Phase 4: personal models. Fitted water coefficients, regularized fitness–fatigue constants, the personal response finder and the prediction log.

This brief is for education and personal training decisions, not medical diagnosis. Sudden or persistent changes in resting heart rate, oxygen saturation or breathing, or any symptoms, belong with a clinician.
